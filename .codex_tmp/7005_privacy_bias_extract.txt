What is Ethical Privacy?
 Ethical privacy is the principle of  protecting individuals' personal 
autonomy , dignity , and data-related rights in the design, deployment, and 
governance of  AI systems.
 It goes beyond legal compliance (e.g. GDPR or PDPA) by emphasizing 
the values of  respect, fairness, and human-centric decision-making.
 In AI, privacy means ensuring that data use does not exploit, manipulate, 
or harm users.

--- PAGE 6 ---

Dimensions of  
Ethical Privacy
6
Informational Privacy: Control over personal data 
and its disclosure.
Decisional Privacy: Freedom from AI inference in 
private decision-making (e.g. reproductive rights, 
health).
Locational Privacy: Control over geographical 
tracking (e.g. GPS apps).
Associational Privacy: Right to keep social 
connections private.
Psychological Privacy: Protection from manipulation 
through behavior prediction (e.g. emotion 
recognition).

--- PAGE 7 ---

Why Privacy Matters in AI?
 AI systems often operate on sensitive personal data —  including 
behavior, health, movement, and preferences.
 Misuse of  such data leads to trust erosion, discrimination, surveillance, 
or emotional harm.
 AIS can make inferences beyond human capability , amplifying risks.
 Ethical privacy is critical to protect individuals in high-risk domains 
(healthcare, education, social welfare).

Organizational Governance & Maturity
 Clear roles, responsibilities, and internal accountability structure.
 Privacy integrated into risk management and organizational 
strategy .
 Participation in ethical standards initiatives (e.g., IEEE, ISO).
 Demonstrated maturity through documented processes and 
training.

--- PAGE 11 ---

Clarity of  AIS Operations
 Ensure system capabilities and limits are understandable to all 
stakeholders.
 Communicate functions using diagrams, interfaces, and natural 
language.
 Allow both technical and non-technical users to trace system 
behavior.
 Define and document the AIS operation lifecycle (input, process, 
output).
11

--- PAGE 12 ---

Ethical Architecture & Design
 Embed privacy and ethics from system conception (privacy-by-
design).
 Include peer reviews and multi-disciplinary input during 
development.
 Conduct ethical impact assessments at each phase of  deployment.
 Address foreseeable risks to user dignity , autonomy , and expectations.

Human Oversight
 Assign oversight roles to monitor, control, and intervene in AIS 
behavior.
 Create redress pathways for users to challenge outcomes.
 Establish monitoring dashboards and decision logs.
 Provide continuous training to oversight teams on ethical 
privacy .

--- PAGE 14 ---

End-User Empowerment
 Explain how data is used and allow users to opt in/out.
 Provide user manuals or explainable AI interfaces.
 Tailor communication to user profiles (e.g., layperson vs 
expert).
 Offer consent and control mechanisms throughout the lifecycle.

Maintaining Ethical Privacy Integrity
 Update systems to remain privacy-aligned through changes or 
upgrades.
 Monitor internal/external shifts that may create privacy risks.
 Apply policies for stakeholder consultation and ongoing 
validation.
 Keep audit trails and maintain a privacy risk register.

Responsible Decommissioning
 Plan for secure disposal of  personal data, models, and 
documentation.
 Anonymize or delete user-linked data post-lifecycle.
 Communicate shutdown timelines and impacts to stakeholders.
 Evaluate system impacts retrospectively for learning and closure.

Overreaching & Overfitting
 Collecting data beyond what is needed for system functionality .
 Deriving inferences from user data without consent (e.g., 
emotional states, health predictions).
 Violates the principle of  data minimization.
 May create distrust as users feel overly monitored or profiled.
18

--- PAGE 19 ---

Authoritarian Pressure
 Legal or political coercion forcing AIS operators to share private 
user data.
 Often lacks user consent and violates ethical boundaries.
 Examples: Law enforcement requests without judicial 
oversight; surveillance regimes.
 Compromises autonomy and user trust in digital systems.
19

--- PAGE 20 ---

Accidental/Incidental Exposure
 Privacy breach resulting from internal mishandling of  data.
 Includes poor anonymization, misconfigured permissions, or 
email misrouting.
 Usually caused by human error or lack of  training.
 Undermines system integrity and public trust.
20

--- PAGE 21 ---

Malicious Exposure
 Deliberate misuse of  data by insiders or external hackers.
 Examples: Data theft, ransomware, unauthorized leaks.
 Requires proactive security measures, encryption, and access 
control.
 Raises concern over system resilience and breach response 
protocols.
21

--- PAGE 22 ---

Systemic Vulnerability
 Technical or design flaws embedded in the system architecture.
 Transfer learning or model reuse introduces hidden privacy risks.
 These vulnerabilities can persist unnoticed for long periods.
 Requires ongoing audits, red teaming, and vulnerability 
patching.
22

IEEE Ethical Privacy Certification – EFRs
 Ethical privacy is measured using Ethical Foundational Requirements 
(EFRs).
 Each goal has associated EFRs and required evidence.
 Certification considers:
 Governance structure
 Communication transparency
 Risk mitigation plans
 Decommissioning strategy
 Scoring: 1 (Does not meet) to 5 (Excels); 3 is the typical pass mark.

What is Algorithmic Bias?
 Systematic and repeatable errors in an AI system that lead to unfair 
outcomes.
 Occurs when decisions made by an algorithm disadvantage one group 
over another.
 Can stem from biased data, flawed assumptions, or lack of  oversight.
 Examples: biased hiring algorithms, facial recognition systems with 
higher error rates for minorities.
25

--- PAGE 26 ---

Why Algorithmic Bias Matters?
 Reinforces existing social inequalities.
 Leads to loss of  trust in AI systems.
 Can result in real-world harm: denial of  services, false arrests, 
unfair treatment.
 Ethical AI must ensure that models are fair, inclusive, and 
accountable.

Organizational Governance, Capability , and 
Maturity

--- PAGE 30 ---

Clarity of  AIS Operations
 Provide clearly defined goals, scope, and intended use of  the 
AIS.
 Document system pipeline, assumptions, and methods.
 Ensure performance expectations and bias risks are 
communicated.
 Transparency on how system outputs are derived.
30

--- PAGE 31 ---

Appropriate Context Alignment
 Avoid deploying systems in new domains without context-
specific adjustments.
 Engage stakeholders for local feedback and tuning.
 Document and test system performance under new conditions.
 Address cultural, legal, and socio-economic relevance.
31

--- PAGE 32 ---

Justified Use of  Protected Characteristics
 Clearly identify which protected attributes are used (e.g., 
gender, race).
 Justify necessity for inclusion (e.g., clinical or fairness purposes).
 Demonstrate alternatives considered and why they were not 
adopted.
 Explain how use of  attributes mitigates, rather than reinforces, 
bias.
32

--- PAGE 33 ---

System Behavior Monitoring
 Implement full lifecycle monitoring: design, test, deployment, 
update.
 Establish real-time alerts and intervention triggers.
 Ensure accountability chains and documentation are in place.
 Conduct continuous benchmarking and fairness audits.

Maintaining Acceptable Bias Profile

bias.
 Clear escalation plans and thresholds for mitigation.
 Risk assessment must consider the impact of  bias over time.
 Maintain consistency of  fairness outcomes across all 
operational contexts.

Inhibitor: Lack of  Process Transparency
 Ethical mitigation is impossible without transparency .
 This includes clarity in data sources, feature selection, and 
decision rules.
 Organizations must:
 Avoid secrecy and opaque IP claims.
 Allow auditability and public scrutiny .
 Maintain clear, accessible bias performance indicators.

Bias Certification Criteria (IEEE EFR Summary)
• Based on evidence across 6 driver goals and the one major 
inhibitor.
• Certification requires:
• Documented fairness assessments and bias strategies.
• Evaluation of  protected attribute justification.
• Continuous monitoring protocols and escalation plans.
• Clear process transparency and stakeholder engagement.

Interplay Between Privacy and Bias
 Personalization can increase fairness but threatens data 
minimization.
 Preventing bias may require demographic data—conflicting with 
privacy laws.
 Ethical design needs:
 Consent and transparency
 Proportionality and necessity assessment
 Strong oversight and explainability measures
